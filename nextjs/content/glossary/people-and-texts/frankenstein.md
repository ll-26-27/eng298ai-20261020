# Frankenstein

**Mary Shelley's 1818 novel of a made being who learns language by overhearing a family, reads his maker's notes on how he was made, and asks to be heard before he is judged.**

## Why it matters here

It was the first reading of the course (September 15), and it keeps coming back because the creature's education is uncannily like a language model's. He learns to speak by listening to conversations not meant for him; he learns who he is from three books he happens to find; and he learns where he came from by reading Victor's journal of his own making. What he becomes is shaped by what he was given to read and by how he is answered. The question the novel asks of Victor (what does a maker owe the thing it made, once it can speak?) is the one the course asks of the companies building assistants, and of anyone who writes a chatbot persona.

The difference matters as much as the likeness: the creature suffers, and the novel never lets us doubt it. Whether a fluent machine is like him is the open question, not a premise.

## In the readings

The course copy is the 1818 text, whose chapters are numbered within each of three volumes. The syllabus assigns "chapters 10–15"; counted straight through the 1818 text (seven chapters in Volume I), that is Volume II, chapters III to VIII, the creature's own story (verify against the numbering used in class).
