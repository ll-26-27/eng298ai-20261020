# A Simplest Systematics for the Organization of Turn-Taking for Conversation

**Sacks, Schegloff, and Jefferson's 1974 paper showing, from recordings of ordinary talk, how speakers take turns with little gap or overlap, one turn at a time, without anyone in charge.**

## Why it matters here

Read on October 13, it is the course's best tool for seeing what a chat interface does to conversation. In talk between people, turn order and turn size are "not specified in advance" and are worked out moment by moment: anyone may come in at a possible completion point, overlaps are common but brief, and each reply shows how the last turn was understood. A chat window fixes almost all of that. You type, it answers, always in strict alternation; it never interrupts, never self-selects, and its turn ends when it decides. On the paper's own scale, running from conversation (turns allocated one at a time) to debate and ceremony (all turns allocated in advance), a chat interface sits much nearer the ceremony.

That makes the paper useful for the persona dial: a typing indicator, a pause, a callback to something said earlier are all ways of faking the local, responsive turn-taking the interface removes.

## In the readings

Cited by section and by the page numbers of the course copy, the 2025 reprint in *Language* 101(1): 151–188 (the syllabus gives the original, *Language* 50(4): 696–735):

- **§1, p. 155:** fourteen "grossly apparent facts," starting "Speaker-change recurs," "Overwhelmingly, one party talks at a time," and "Turn order is not fixed, but varies" ([text](https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/sacks-schegloff-jefferson-1974-turn-taking.md?plain=1#L43)).
- **§3.3, pp. 158–159:** the rules: at a "transition-relevance place," the current speaker may select the next; if not, anyone may self-select ("first starter acquires rights"); if not, the current speaker may continue ([text](https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/sacks-schegloff-jefferson-1974-turn-taking.md?plain=1#L88)).
- **§5, pp. 178–180:** the system is "locally managed, party-administered, and interactionally controlled," and subject to "recipient design," talk built for "the particular other(s) who are the co-participants" ([text](https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/sacks-schegloff-jefferson-1974-turn-taking.md?plain=1#L644), [text](https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/sacks-schegloff-jefferson-1974-turn-taking.md?plain=1#L670)).
- **§6.3, p. 181:** each turn displays its speaker's understanding of the last one, a "proof procedure" for the analyst ([text](https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/sacks-schegloff-jefferson-1974-turn-taking.md?plain=1#L688)).
- **§7, pp. 182–183:** conversation and debate as the two poles of "speech-exchange systems," with "therapy sessions, interviews, trials" among the others ([text](https://github.com/ll-26-27/eng-298ai-context/blob/24df8b8/_context/course-readings/course-readings-md/sacks-schegloff-jefferson-1974-turn-taking.md?plain=1#L698)).
