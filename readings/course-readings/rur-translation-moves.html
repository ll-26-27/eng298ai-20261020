R.U.R. in translation

The 1923 English acting edition (Paul Selver, adapted by Nigel Playfair) set against Čapek’s Czech of 1920: the cuts, additions and quiet changes of meaning, read 1,000 words at a time by ten readers.

The English renumbers the play. Čapek’s Předehra (prologue) becomes Act One, his three acts become Acts Two and Three, and his Act Three survives only as a shortened “Epilogue.”

Section 01: Předehra part 1/3 ↔ English Act One

Czech from the cast list "OSOBY" through Domin's "výrobek inženýra je technicky vytříbenější než výrobek přírody"; English from "Description of Characters" through Domin's "The product of an engineer _is_ technically at a higher pitch of perfection than a product of Nature."

The acting edition keeps Domin's history-of-the-invention monologue largely intact but turns the scene into a flirtation, adding stage business (flowers, candy, a cushion, a hand-kiss) that the Czech never has. It cuts the sexual and the most irreverent material, drops Čapek's jokes about age, names and form of address, and recasts the cast list as a set of casting notes, removing Busman's description as a Jew altogether.

cut

Busman's antisemitic tag and the whole Czech cast description replaced

Čapek, 1920
Busman: tlustý, plešatý, krátkozraký žid

“Busman: fat, bald, short-sighted Jew”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
(cut)

Busman has no entry at all in the English "Description of Characters", even though he enters later in the act. The English list is rewritten as casting notes ("A handsome man of 35. Forceful, efficient and humorous at times."), and the Czech remark that everyone is ten years older in the play proper is dropped.

addition

Robots are humanised in the cast note; Sulla is "pathetic"

Čapek, 1920
Sulla: Robotka … Roboti v předehře oblečeni jako lidé. Jsou úsečná v pohybech i výslovnosti, bezvýrazných tváří, upřeného pohledu.

“Sulla: a female Robot ("Robotka") … The Robots in the prologue are dressed like people. They are curt in movement and speech, with expressionless faces and a fixed stare.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
SULLA: _A pathetic figure. Young, pretty and attractive._ … _with the exception of_ SULLA, HELENA _and_ PRIMUS, _who convey a touch of humanity._

In the Czech, Sulla is just "Robotka", and every Robot moves mechanically. The English exempts Sulla, Helena and Primus and makes Sulla sentimental and sexualised ("pretty and attractive"). This telegraphs the play's ending from the first page.

stage direction

Domin's lecture turned into a courtship with added props and a hand-kiss

Čapek, 1920
Domin: Tak tedy mladý Rossum si řekl: Člověk, to je něco, co dejme tomu cítí radost, hraje na housle, chce jít na procházku

“So young Rossum said to himself: Man is something that, let's say, feels joy, plays the violin, wants to go for a walk”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
"A man is something that feels happy, plays the piano, likes going for a walk…" … Do you play the piano? HELENA. Yes. DOMIN. That's good. (_Kisses her hand. She lowers her head._)

The violin becomes a piano so that Domin can ask about it and kiss her hand. Throughout this stretch the English adds flowers, candy, a cigarette case, a phone call ("I'm in conference"), cushion-fixing, laughter, and a distracted Helena who doesn't hear his question ("_Absently_ What?"). None of this is in the Czech. Domin's rapture is in the Czech ("uchvácen"), but only in what he says, never in what he does.

politics/religion

Sex glands and the inventory of useless organs cut

Čapek, 1920
Slepé střevo, mandle, pupek, samé zbytečnosti. Dokonce i--hm--i pohlavní žlázy. / Helena: Ale ty přece--ty přece-- / Domin:--nejsou zbytečné, já vím.

“Appendix, tonsils, navel, nothing but superfluities. Even--hm--even the sex glands. / But surely those--those-- / --aren't superfluous, I know.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
(cut)

This is the scene's only sexual joke, and the edition removes all of it. That includes Domin's hint that the Robots are deliberately made without sex, which matters later in the play. Only "he then decided to manufacture everything as in the human body" is left.

politics/religion

"Dethrone God" softened to "substitute for God"

Čapek, 1920
Víte, chtěl jaksi vědecky sesadit Boha. … Nešlo mu o nic víc než podat důkaz, že nebylo žádného Pánaboha zapotřebí.

“You know, he wanted, in a way, to scientifically depose God. … All he wanted was to prove that there had been no need for any Lord God.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
He wanted to become a sort of scientific substitute for _God_. … His sole purpose was nothing more or less than to prove that God was no longer necessary.

"Sesadit" (to depose, to dethrone) is an act of overthrowing; "substitute" makes Rossum a rival rather than a regicide. The colloquial, slightly mocking "Pánbůh" becomes plain "God", and "there had been no need" becomes "no longer necessary". Both changes take some of the sting out of the atheism.

cut

Young Rossum's "new age" slogan and the Indians/docents/idiots joke cut

Čapek, 1920
Myslel, že udělá skutečné lidi, tedy snad nějaké nové Indiány, docenty nebo idioty, víte? … Mladý Rossum, slečno, to byl nový věk. Věk výroby po věku poznání.

“He thought he'd make real people, perhaps some new Indians, lecturers or idiots, you know? … Young Rossum, miss, was a new age. The age of production after the age of knowledge.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
(cut)

Two pointed lines go: the satirical list (the Indians/docents/idiots joke, which sounds the same in Czech) and the thesis of the speech, production replacing knowledge. The edition also drops "the old man would have done well at a university" and "starý Rossum neměl trochy vkusu".

wordplay/names

Domin's slips with Helena's name and the "nedůvěřujete / neobyčejně" pun lost

Čapek, 1920
Helena: Vy mi nedůvěřujete? / Domin: Neobyčejně, slečno hele--pardon, slečno Gloryová. Vskutku neobyčejně potěšen--

“You don't trust me? / Extraordinarily, Miss Hele--pardon, Miss Glory. Truly extraordinarily pleased--”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
HELENA. But what is it? What's the matter? / DOMIN. I'm remarkably pleased.

In the Czech, "Neobyčejně" first sounds like an answer to "you don't trust me?" (extraordinarily!), and Domin nearly calls her by her first name. Later he does say "Přibližně, slečno Heleno", and the English makes that "Approximately--Miss Glory". The English removes both the comic misfire and every sign of his growing familiarity in how he addresses her.

reassignment

Helena's comeback in the interruption game cut and replaced

Čapek, 1920
Domin: Slečno Gloryová, je pro nás neobyčejnou ctí, že--že-- / Helena:--že vám nemůžeme ukázat dveře.

“Miss Glory, it is an extraordinary honour for us that--that-- / --that we can't show you the door.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
HELENA. If you only knew how enormously that-- / DOMIN. Interests you. Europe's talking about nothing else.

In the Czech, Helena once turns Domin's habit of finishing her sentences back on him with a sharp joke. The English cuts her retort and adds another sentence that Domin finishes for her, so he keeps the upper hand and she loses her one witty line.

addition

Added explanations: "thirst for life", "sown", and "what a Robot looks like inside"

Čapek, 1920
Ale protože neměl kousku humoru, vzal si do hlavy, že udělá normálního obratlovce nebo snad člověka. A tak se do toho pustil.

“But because he hadn't a scrap of humour, he took it into his head to make a normal vertebrate or perhaps a man. And so he set about it.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
This artificial living _matter_ of his had a raging thirst for life. It didn't mind being sown or mixed together. That couldn't be done with natural albumen.

These lines aren't in this Czech text. They may come from the earlier Czech edition Selver worked from, and "sown" looks like a slip for "sewn" (sešívat). After "no soul" the English also adds an exchange the Czech doesn't have ("How do you know they have no soul?" / "Have you ever seen what a Robot looks like inside?"), which sets up Domin's closing line.

meaning shift

Smaller shifts: philosopher → physiologist, rotifer → beetle, diesel → gasoline

Čapek, 1920
starý Rossum veliký filozof … počínajíc vířníkem … Naftový motor nemá mít třapce a ornamenty

“old Rossum, the great philosopher … beginning with the rotifer … A diesel (oil) motor must not have tassels and ornaments”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
old Rossum, the great physiologist … beginning with some sort of a beetle … A gasoline motor must not have tassels or ornaments

Making Rossum a "physiologist" loses the irony of a philosopher turned materialist. The rotifer, a microscopic animal at the bottom of the tree of life, becomes an imprecise beetle. "Gasoline" and "yards" Americanise the speech. Also cut: the "tečka" (full stop) that Domin says aloud in his breathless recitation, the abbreviation "Patnáct tisíc R.", and most of the poster slogans ("Každý si kup svého Robota!").

Section 02: Předehra part 2/3 ↔ English Act One

Czech from "Helena: Říká se, že člověk je výrobek boží." to Domin's "co je na světě církví a bláznů."; English Act One from "But man is supposed to be the product of God." to "...churches and idiots there are in the world."

The acting edition keeps the dialogue skeleton but cuts most of Domin's factory exposition (the assembly line, "school", the daily output) and adds visual business of its own: a parade of labour Robots, a longer comic ship-spec routine for Sulla, and a lot of flirtation (flowers, kissed hands, candy, arm-in-arm). The result is lighter and more romantic, and Čapek's satire of mass production is thinner.

cut

Factory tour and Robot "schooling" cut

Čapek, 1920
Pak je montovna, kde se to dává dohromady, víte, jako automobily. Každý dělník přidělá jen jednu součást [...] Mohli by docela dobře učit na univerzitách.

“Then there's the assembly room where it's put together, you know, like automobiles. Each worker adds just one part [...] They could quite well teach at universities.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
(cut)

The English stops at the spinning mill and drops the Fordist assembly line, the "drying room where fresh products work", the "finishing" that equals human "school", the parrot memory ("never invent anything new"), the jibe about university teaching, and "fifteen thousand pieces a day" with the defective ones sent to the stamping mill. Most of the play's industrial and academic satire in this scene goes with it.

reassignment

Helena's apology becomes her request to change the subject

Čapek, 1920
Helena: Zlobíte se na mne? / Domin: Ale chraň bůh! Myslím jen, že .. že jsme mohli mluvit o jiných věcech.

“Helena: Are you angry with me? / Domin: Heaven forbid! I only think that... that we could have talked about other things.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
HELENA. (_Watching his gestures_) Mayn't we talk about something else? / DOMIN. Perhaps it would be better.

In Czech, Domin himself breaks off his recital and wishes they had talked about something else. The English hands that wish to Helena as a request, which makes him less self-aware and her more squeamish, and it covers the cut above.

addition

Added demonstration of "manual labor" Robots

Čapek, 1920
Máme jemnější a hrubší Roboti. Ti lepší budou snad žít dvacet let.

“We have finer and coarser Robots. The better ones will live perhaps twenty years.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
Marius, bring in samples of the manual labor Robot. [...] There you are, as powerful as a small tractor. Guaranteed to have average intelligence.

The acting edition adds a whole piece of stage business, "specimens of the two extremes", with Marius leading in two labour Robots that grind to "a mechanical standstill". It then tags Sulla as "a Robot, best grade" so she becomes the other extreme. Čapek's Robots stay offstage until Sulla appears.

addition

Sulla's ship patter inflated into a comic routine

Čapek, 1920
Sulla: Dvacet uzlů za hodinu. Tonáž dvanáct tisíc.

“Twenty knots an hour. Tonnage twelve thousand.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
SULLA. Forty knots an hour. Fifty thousand tons. One of the latest vessels, Miss Glory. [...] A crew of fifteen hundred, Captain Harpy, eight boilers--

The figures are doubled or more, and the English adds a crew count, an invented "Captain Harpy" and boilers. The barometer reading "705" is dropped. Sulla's mechanical recall becomes a running gag that Domin has to cut off, and the English adds a showy "Oui! Madame!" before the four-language letter openings, where "Ctěný pane" loses its diacritics as "Cteny pane".

meaning shift

"Just as good people as we are" loses "people"

Čapek, 1920
Roboti jsou stejně dobří lidé jako my.

“Robots are just as good people as we are.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
Robots are just as good as we are.

Helena's Czech claim is that Robots are *people* ("lidé"), which sets up the irony a few lines later. The English drops the noun and turns an ontological claim into a vague one about worth.

meaning shift

"You are people like us" becomes "living creatures"

Čapek, 1920
Proboha, vždyť jste lidé jako my, jako celá Evropa, jako celý svět!

“For God's sake, you are people just like us, like all Europe, like the whole world!”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
Good heavens, you are living creatures, just like us, like the whole of Europe, like the whole world.

The joke depends on Helena telling the human directors "you are people like us" while she thinks they are Robots, and on Domin answering in the same words ("lidé jako vy. Jako celá Evropa"). The English keeps Domin's "human beings just like us" but changes Helena's half to "living creatures", which blunts the echo.

cut

Helena's defiance and Gall's protective outburst cut

Čapek, 1920
Helena: Ne, nedomluvila. Leda byste mi násilím zavřel ústa. / Dr. Gall: Harry, opovažte se! / Helena: Děkuji vám. Věděla jsem, že mne budete chránit.

“Helena: No, I haven't finished. Unless you shut my mouth by force. / Dr. Gall: Harry, don't you dare! / Helena: Thank you. I knew you would protect me.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
DOMIN. Just a minute. I'm afraid that Miss Glory is of the opinion she has been talking to Robots. / HELEN. Of course.

The English cuts Helena's challenge to Domin and Gall's use of Domin's first name. Domin also states her mistake outright, where in Czech he asks it as a question ("Jste si tím jista, že mluvíte s Roboty?"). Helena loses a moment of spirit and gets an easy laugh line instead.

stage direction

Flirtation added in stage directions

Čapek, 1920
(Fabry, Busman a Dr. Gall přitahují lenošky)

“(Fabry, Busman and Dr. Gall pull up armchairs)”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
(_As they are introduced they rise and come C. to kiss her hand [...]_) BUSMAN. (_Handing flowers_) Allow me, Miss Glory. [...] FABRY. (_Handing candy_) Please, Miss Glory. DOMIN. Will you have a cigarette, Miss Glory?

Typical of the 1923 acting edition, it adds courtship business that has no basis in the Czech: Helena gives Domin a flower for his buttonhole, he takes her hand and walks her arm-in-arm, the men tidy their trousers and ties, kiss her hand, and offer flowers, candy and a cigarette. The romance comes much earlier and Helena's mission matters less.

mistranslation

Directors' titles altered; Gall becomes a psychologist

Čapek, 1920
inženýr Fabry, generální technický ředitel [...] Doktor Gall, přednosta fyziologického a výzkumného oddělení.

“engineer Fabry, general technical director [...] Doctor Gall, head of the physiological and research department.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
this is Doctor Fabry, General Technical Manager; [...] Doctor Gall, head of the Psychological and Experimental Department; and Alquist, head of the Building Department, R. U. R.

Gall's department changes from "physiological" to "Psychological", which duplicates Hallemeier's psychology institute and muddles who later alters the Robots' physiology. Engineer Fabry becomes "Doctor". The order of introductions changes, and Alquist gets a self-deprecating added line, "Just a builder."

tone/register

Helena's rolled "hrrozné" and the god-oaths flattened

Čapek, 1920
Helena: To je hrrozné! [...] Busman: Jemináčku, to je sláva! [...] Busman: Ale božíčku, pročpak ne?

“That's terrrible! [...] Busman: Jeepers (lit. "little Jesus-Mary"), what a glory! [...] Busman: Oh, little God, why ever not?”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
HELENA. How dreadful! [...] BUSMAN. By Jove, that's fine. [...] BUSMAN. Why not?

Čapek's spelling "hrrozné/hrrozní" marks Helena's affected, girlish emphasis, and it disappears. Busman's homely diminutive oaths ("Jemináčku", "božíčku") become stock English "By Jove" or vanish. A pun may also be lost: "to je sláva" ("what glory") next to the name Glory.

other

Lines reordered around the "saviours and prophets" speech

Čapek, 1920
Helena: Jsem hloupé děvče. Teď--teď mne pošlete první lodí zpátky. [...] Helena: Protože už víte--protože--protože bych vám bouřila Roboty.

“I'm a silly girl. Now--now you'll send me back on the first ship. [...] Because now you know--because--because I'd stir up your Robots against you.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
HELENA. (_Seriously_) But you know I've come to disturb your Robots for you. [...] HELENA. I'm a stupid girl. Send me back by the first ship.

The English moves Helena's self-reproach after Domin's mocking speech, and the line about inciting the Robots to before it. "Bouřit" (to rouse to revolt) softens to "disturb". Domin's "hundreds" ("na sta") of saviours shrinks to "close upon a hundred", and a stage direction, "Mocking her serious tone", spells out his condescension.

Section 03: Předehra part 3/3 ↔ English Act One

Czech from "Helena: A vy je necháte mluvit k Robotům?" to "OPONA" (end of the prologue); English Act One from "HELENA. And yet you let them speak to the Robots." to "*The curtain falls quickly*".

The acting edition follows the debate line by line but trims Domin's utopian speech of its Communist slogan, its biblical Adam apostrophe and its warning of "terrible things", while adding a Christ-like image, a liturgical "Amen" and a speech giving Helena the idea of robot revolt. The proposal scene is turned into a conventional romantic curtain: Helena's insults and comic rolled r's go, and stage directions supply the kiss and the congratulations.

politics/religion

Domin's utopia loses its slogan, its Adam and its warning, and gains a Gospel image

Čapek, 1920
Nyní ber každý, kolik potřebuješ. Není bídy. ... Předtím snad přijdou strašlivé věci, slečno Gloryová. ... Adame, Adame! už nebudeš jíst chléb svůj v potu tváře

“Now let each take as much as he needs. There is no want. ... Before that, terrible things may come, Miss Glory. ... Adam, Adam! no more shalt thou eat thy bread in the sweat of thy brow”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
There will be no poverty. ... Nobody will get bread at the cost of life and hatred. The Robots will wash the feet of the beggar and prepare a bed for him in his house.

The quasi-Marxist "from each/to each" line, the foreshadowing of catastrophe, and the Genesis apostrophe (with "you will return to paradise, where the Lord's hand fed you") are all cut. In their place comes a feet-washing image found nowhere in the Czech. The politics come out milder, and the old Testament curse is swapped for a New Testament picture of charity.

addition

"Amen. So be it." added as a response

Čapek, 1920
Domin: ... Budeš pánem stvoření. / Helena: Zmátl jste mne.

“Thou shalt be lord of creation. / Helena: You have confused me.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
He will be _Lord_ of creation. / BUSMAN. Amen. / FABRY. So be it. / HELENA. (_Rises_) You have bewildered me.

Two invented responses make Domin's speech into a creed with a congregation. This plays up its religious overtones even though the Adam passage has been cut.

addition

Helena is handed the revolt idea

Čapek, 1920
Helena: Ne, ne, to je duše! / Fabry: Myslíte, že duše začíná skřípáním zubů? / Domin: To se odstraní, slečno Gloryová.

“Helena: No, no, that's the soul! / Fabry: Do you think the soul begins with gnashing of teeth? / Domin: That will be removed, Miss Glory.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
HELENA. Perhaps it's just a sign that there's a struggle within. Perhaps it's a sort of revolt. Oh, if you could infuse them with it.

The English adds a whole speech that names "revolt" and has Helena wish the robots had it. This spells out the uprising to come, which Čapek leaves implicit in "Robot's Cramp".

addition

Beer, bossing and "intelligent" robots added to Hallemeier's catechism

Čapek, 1920
Hallemeier: Aha. Mají snad hlasovat? Nemají dokonce dostávat mzdu? ... Jsou to jen Roboti. Bez vlastní vůle. Bez vášní. Bez dějin. Bez duše.

“Aha. Are they supposed to vote? Shouldn't they even get wages? ... They are only Robots. Without a will of their own. Without passions. Without history. Without a soul.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
HALLEMEIER. Aha! I suppose they're to vote. To drink beer. To order us about? / HELENA. Why shouldn't they drink beer? ... They are only workmen. / HELENA. Oh, but they're so intelligent.

The beer joke and "order us about" are new, and they make the gag a class joke about workers getting above themselves. "Only Robots" becomes "only workmen", which ties robots to the working class, and the striking "without history" is dropped.

meaning shift

"Most alien" becomes "hardest to like"

Čapek, 1920
Fabry: Nemožno, slečno Gloryová. Nic není člověku cizejšího než Robot.

“Impossible, Miss Glory. Nothing is more alien to man than a Robot.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
FABRY. Impossible, Miss Glory! _Nothing is harder to like than a Robot._

Čapek's claim is ontological: robots are alien to humans. The English makes it a matter of likeability, which shrinks the philosophical point to a quip.

cut

Fabry's "birth by machine" cut

Čapek, 1920
Fabry: Prosím za odpuštění. Je to veliký pokrok rodit strojem. Je to pohodlnější a rychlejší. Každé zrychlení je pokrok, slečno.

“I beg your pardon. It is great progress to give birth by machine. It is more convenient and faster. Every acceleration is progress, miss.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
(cut)

The English keeps the remark about childhood but drops the sentence on reproduction, and with it Fabry's embarrassed "pardon" before saying it in front of a lady. The theme of sterility that drives the later acts is muted here.

cut

The "Robotka" exchange and the insults cut from the proposal

Čapek, 1920
Helena: To je hrrozné! Vemte si nějakou Robotku. / Domin: Není žena. ... Helena: Vy jste surovec! / Domin: To nic není. Muž má být trochu surovec.

“That's terrrible! Marry some Robotess. / Domin: She's not a woman. ... Helena: You're a brute! / Domin: That's nothing. A man should be a bit of a brute.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
(cut)

Both cuts lose Czech wordplay. "Robotka" is the feminine of Robot, and Domin's flat "she's not a woman" answers it. The brute/madman parallel is cut down to the madman line alone, which softens Domin's coercive edge.

tone/register

Helena's comic rolled r's flattened

Čapek, 1920
Helena: Oh, to je--hrrozné! ... Ne, ne! Prosím vás, pusťte! Vždyť mne rrrozmačkáte!

“Oh, that's--terrrible! ... No, no! Please, let go! You'll crrrush me!”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
HELENA. Oh, that's terrible. ... HELENA. Don't, you're hurting me!

Čapek spells Helena's affected, girlish trill into the text. The English drops it, and "you'll crush me" (playful protest) becomes "you're hurting me", which on the page reads more darkly.

stage direction

Kiss and congratulations written in by stage directions

Čapek, 1920
Helena: (brání se) Za nic na světě--Ale Harry! / (Zaklepání. ...)

“Helena: (resisting) Not for anything in the world--But Harry!”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
HELENA. But--but-- (_He embraces her; kisses her. She embraces him. KNOCKING at R. door._) ... (_He embraces her. The MEN rush around them and offer congratulations._)

In the Czech, Helena gives in only by blurting his first name while still resisting. The acting edition spells it out with a mutual embrace, "(_Not meaning it_)", a "WARN Curtain" cue and an ensemble congratulation tableau.

other

Busman's numbers and idioms adjusted

Čapek, 1920
Sto dvacet dolarů ošacený kus ... Můj ty Tondo ... všechny továrny praskají jako žaludy ... vy nevíte, co to je příliš drahý krajíc. Pro kulturu a tak dále.

“A hundred and twenty dollars a clothed piece ... My dear Tonda [folk exclamation] ... all factories are bursting like acorns ... you don't know what a too-expensive slice of bread is. For culture and so on.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
a hundred and fifty dollars each, fully dressed ... Good gracious ... All factories will go pop like chestnuts ... Miss Glory, you don't realize that even _that's_ too expensive.

The price changes from $120 to $150 for no clear reason. The Czech idioms are naturalised (acorns become chestnuts). Busman's sneer that cheap bread matters "for culture" is lost, so his line now says nearly the opposite of the Czech.

04

Section 04: Dějství první part 1/3 ↔ English Act Two

Czech from the act's opening stage direction ("Helenin salón") to Helena calling Alquist up and going to meet him; English Act Two from the opening SCENE to Helena's "You look very nice in your mason's overalls."

The English follows the scene's order closely but systematically removes its religious and procreative charge: Nana's sermons, the curse of barrenness, and Helena's own childlessness all vanish. In their place the acting edition adds stage business, political headlines and a line in which Helena excuses the firm, making the scene more about war news and less about a sin against God.

cut

Helena's childlessness cut entirely

Čapek, 1920

Section 05: Dějství první part 2/3 ↔ English Act Two

Czech from "(Pauza)" / Helena returning with Alquist "jako zedník" to Helena kneeling at the fire with the yellowed papers; English from "Oh, Mr. Alquist. … come just as you are" to "Is it burning, Nana? All this has got to be burned."

The acting edition roughly halves this stretch. Čapek, 1920

Section 06: Dějství první part 3/3 ↔ English Act Two

Czech from "Nána: (vstane) Co je to?" (burning of Rossum's manuscript) to "OPONA" after "Poplach Robotů. Útok."; English Act Two from NANA "What's that?" through the final *Curtain*.

The English keeps the plot beats and most of the dialogue but trims Čapek's verbal play (the drinking song, Nana's proverbs, Hallemeier's tipsy riffs) and cuts Alquist from the scene entirely. In their place it adds expository and ironic lines (the Robots' seizure of arms and radio, Gall's boast that the Robots could never win, Domin's "So humanity will be safe") and much more stage business, ending on a staged tableau with steel shutters and Helena in Domin's arms.

Section 07: Dějství druhé part 1/2 ↔ English Act Three

Hallemeier: Hlouposti. Nikdo není vinen. Zkrátka Roboti--Inu, Roboti se nějak změnili. Copak někdo může za Roboty? ... Alquist: Já žaluju vědu! žaluju techniku! Domina! sebe! nás všechny!

Selver & Playfair, 1923
HALLEMEIER. Nobody is to blame except the Robots. / ALQUIST. No, it is _we_ are to blame. You, Domin, myself--all of us.

The English drops Alquist's "rivulets of blood on every threshold" vision, his indictment of science and technology, and the line about a burial mound of human bones bigger than any Genghis Khan built. It also flips Hallemeier's "nobody is to blame" into "nobody except the Robots". Čapek's charge against technical progress itself shrinks to a vaguer "selfish ends".

Domin's dream gains "mechanical slaves"

Čapek, 1920
Chtěl jsem, abychom z celého lidstva udělali aristokracii světa. Neomezené, svobodné ~ svrchované lidi. A třeba víc než lidi.

I wanted to turn the whole of mankind into an aristocracy of the world. An aristocracy nourished by millions of mechanical slaves. Unrestricted, free and consummated in man. And maybe more than man.

The added sentence states outright the slave-owning logic that Čapek leaves implicit, which hardens Domin's utopia. "Svrchované" (sovereign, supreme) also becomes the muddled "consummated in man".

Čapek, 1920

Selver & Playfair, 1923

Both pointed aphorisms disappear: Helena's defence of folk wisdom against the engineers, and Domin's grim "make stones human and they will stone us". Only the bare "Nobody can hate man more than man" is left, so the exchange loses its bite on both sides.

addition

"The dead [judge] the dead" replaced by ghost melodrama

Čapek, 1920
Domin: Mrtví mrtvého.

Čapek's terse three-word quip on Gall's demand to be judged becomes a run of invented spectral lines and an interruption by Fabry. The adaptation also has Domin hand out everyone's death in one speech ("shot in the head" is added) instead of Čapek's one-word answers to each man's question.

reassignment

Domin and Helena: love-scene added, reproach cut, line reassigned

Čapek, 1920

Domin's cool line about Gall's duties goes to Fabry, and his rebuke of Helena's "recklessness" is cut. Invented endearments and stage directions ("crushes her in his arms", "takes her in his arms") make Domin a romantic lead rather than a manager reproaching his wife.

cut

Busman's "avalanche of demand" and "Ecce homo" speeches cut

Čapek, 1920
historii nedělají veliké sny, ale maličké potřeby všech počestných, mírně zlodějských a sobeckých lidiček ... aby se tím dal člověk vycpat pro muzeum Vesmíru, s nápisem Ejhle, člověk.

Selver & Playfair, 1923
(cut)

The English keeps only "It's the demand that controls the output". It loses Busman's image of the avalanche of orders, his own "Busmanish dream" of a new world economy, and the bitter Ecce homo. The cynical financier's one philosophical speech is gone, and filler lines take its place ("Oh, stop it, Busman." / "Seriously, Harry, I think we might try it.").

wordplay/names

Hallemeier's "twilight of men" and new hedonism cut

Čapek, 1920

“The twilight of men, damn it all! Lads, I'm turning into a sensualist [pleasure-seeker].”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
(cut)

"Soumrak lidí" plays on Götterdämmerung (soumrak bohů). Along with Hallemeier's remark that music "spiritualises, refines" a man, it goes, leaving only "What a fine thing music is". His "člověk se nesmí dát!" (a man mustn't give in) becomes the defiant "Who says we must give in?"

stage direction

Čapek, 1920

“What is Mrs Helena playing? ... Ah, she's still practising?”

Selver & Playfair, 1923

Čapek leaves the music unnamed ("practising something new"), and Alquist's wry wonder that she is still practising at the end of the world becomes a pious "Thank God". The acting edition specifies Rachmaninoff's Elegie and a "MUSIC stops" cue, typical of its production-ready directions.

meaning shift

Ledger sums, voltage and "trained for war" quietly changed

Čapek, 1920

“The Robots are trained for war. ... We've put two thousand volts into the railings ... Three hundred and sixteen million.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
The Robots are practised marksmen. ... I can put twelve hundred volts into that railing. ... Three hundred sixty million.

Busman's running total changes throughout (316 / 370 / 452 / 520 million become 360 / 420 / 520 million), and his cry "half a milliard!" is cut. 2,000 volts becomes 1,200. "Trained for war" narrows to "marksmen". None of these changes serves a clear purpose, which suggests carelessness or a misreading.

addition

Gall's relief "at least they're unarmed" flattened; Domin's panic added

Čapek, 1920

Selver & Playfair, 1923
DR. GALL. They aren't armed. / DOMIN. (_Puzzled_) We couldn't hold our own for five minutes. Man alive, they overwhelm us like an avalanche.

Gall's anxious hopes become flat statements ("Not firearms"), which reads as if Gall knows something. Domin gets new lines of panic ("snap like a match", "in a vise") that are not in Čapek's opening.

tone/register

Busman's familiar address and short sight dropped

Čapek, 1920
Hlavní knihy, dětičky. ... Ne, jenom velikou modrou plochu, jako když mák rozseje. ... Ah tak. Škoda, že na ně nevidím.

“The ledgers, little children. ... No, just a great blue expanse, as if poppies had been sown. ... Oh, I see. A pity I can't see them.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
The ledger, my boy. ... Nothing but blue--blue everywhere.

Busman's avuncular diminutives (dětičky, holenku, lidičky, "chlapík Busman") are flattened to "my boy" or dropped. So are the poppy-field simile and the hint that he is short-sighted. In return, "Páni Roboti, vaše blahorodí" is neatly rendered as the mock-formal "Worthy and Worshipful Robots".

Section 08: Dějství druhé part 2/2 ↔ English Act Three

Czech from "Domlela [Domin]: Rossumův rukopis?" to the curtain ("Roboti, do práce! Marš! OPONA"); English Act Three from "DOMIN. Rossum's manuscript?" to the final "*Curtain*".

The acting edition keeps the plot beats (the vote, the burnt manuscript, Busman's death, the last stand, Radius's proclamation) but cuts the act's lyrical and metaphysical speeches almost entirely, above all the lamp litany, Nana's Judgement Day, and Radius's will-to-power coda. In their place it adds explanatory or theatrical material: a Busman sabotage plot, a Radius "gratitude" exchange, and a march to the footlights. Religion goes, and so does most of the play's large claim about humanity's future.

politics/religion

The lamp litany secularised into "Man's power"

Čapek, 1920
Fabry: (rozsvítí žárovku na krbu) Sviť, hromničko lidstva! ... Alquist: Věčná lampo boží, ohnivý voze, svatá svíce víry, modli se! Oltáři obětní--

“Fabry: (lights the bulb on the fireplace) Shine, little death-candle of mankind! ... Alquist: Eternal lamp of God, fiery chariot, holy candle of faith, pray! Sacrificial altar--”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
FABRY. (_Facing the lamp_) From man's thought and man's power came this light, our last hope. ... ALQUIST. (_Facing lamp_) Man's power.

Čapek wrote a six-voice litany. "Hromnička" is the blessed Candlemas candle lit for the dying, and each man and Helena hails the lamp in his own idiom (science, faith, the cave-fire, the torch, the family's evening lamp). The English cuts Gall's cave-fire, Helena's "Večerní lampa rodiny. Děti, děti, musíte už spát" and the death-candle image. It makes the devout Alquist echo a humanist slogan, "Man's power", the opposite of his speech.

addition

Busman's double-cross scheme added (and the ship named)

Čapek, 1920
Domin: Busmane, žádný podvod! / Busman: (vyskočí) Nesmysl! V zájmu lidstva je--

“Domin: Busman, no cheating! / Busman: (jumps up) Nonsense! In the interest of humanity it is--”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
When we're on board the _Ultimus_ I'll stop up my ears with cotton wool, lie down somewhere in the hold, and you'll train the guns on the factory and blow it to smithereens, and _with_ it Rossum's secret.

The English adds a whole plan to blow up the factory once they are aboard, names the ship (Ultimus does not appear in this Czech text), and gives Fabry a "No!" and Domin "Busman, you're no--gentleman". The insertion gives Busman's later "I may not be a gentleman" (Czech "Já nejsem džentlmen") an antecedent. It may come from a different Czech edition rather than from Playfair.

meaning shift

The dilemma reframed as "enslaving mankind"

Čapek, 1920
Máme prodat výrobu a zachránit lidské duše?, nebo máme ji zničit a--a--a nás všechny s sebou?

“Are we to sell the production and save human souls?, or are we to destroy it and--and--and all of us with it?”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
Are we to sell the secret? And save that many souls at the risk of enslaving mankind--

Čapek's choice is between selling and destroying everyone, themselves included. The English swaps the self-destruction for a political stake, "enslaving mankind", that the Czech states only later and more grandly ("kdo bude mít v rukou výrobu, bude pánem světa"). The escalating crescendo that follows ("Nikdy už lidstvo nebude s Roboty hotovo... Konec dějin lidstva, konec civilizace", each line answered by a shouted "Prodejte!") is cut.

meaning shift

"Barren flower" and Nana's "punishment" replaced by an economic explanation

Čapek, 1920
To... to, že se lidé stali hluchým květem! ... Nána říkala, že to je trest--Všichni, všichni říkali, že se nemohou rodit lidé, protože se dělá tolik Robotů

“That... that people had become a barren flower! ... Nana said it is a punishment--Everyone, everyone said people can't be born because so many Robots are being made”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
That children had stopped being born. Because human beings were not needed to do the work of the world. That's why--

The botanical metaphor ("hluchý květ", a flower that sets no fruit) and Nana's providential reading are both dropped for a rationalised, labour-market cause. Domin's bitter self-indictment "My jsme to myslili... příliš dobře, my lidé" (We meant it... too well, we humans) becomes the conciliatory "Well, perhaps in your own way you are right." The relay of speeches that follows, on the Robots dying out in twenty years and humanity restarting from "a few savages", is cut too.

meaning shift

Fabry's utopia: "a few years" becomes "a few hundred years"

Čapek, 1920
A bůh ví, já věřím, že by za pár let zas mohlo dobývat světa. ... že zplodí bezpočtu hrdin, kteří ponesou svou hořící duši v čele lidí ... znovu snít o dobytí planet a sluncí.

“And God knows, I believe that in a few years it could conquer the world again. ... that it will beget countless heroes who will carry their burning soul at the head of men ... dream again of conquering planets and suns.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
Why, in a few hundred years we could conquer the world again.

The timescale is multiplied by a hundred. Fabry's heroic, cosmic vision (heroes, planets, suns) is cut, along with Busman's Old Testament pastoral wish ("starozákonně, po pastýřsku"). Busman's line becomes the English idiom "That suits me right down to the ground."

addition

Radius spared: gratitude exchange added

Čapek, 1920
Fabry: (skloní revolver) Budiž. / Hallemeier: (hrozí pěstí) Ty pacholku!

“Fabry: (lowers the revolver) So be it. / Hallemeier: (shakes his fist) You scoundrel!”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
FABRY. (_Pause. Lowering the revolver_) Very well. / DOMIN. It was Radius' life I spared. / DR. GALL. Do you think that a Robot can be grateful? (_Pause._)

Hallemeier's curse at Radius is replaced by a new exchange that makes a moral point out loud and ties back to the earlier sparing of Radius. Domin's line is odd, since in this scene Helena is the one who stops the shot. The English also adds Domin's "Radius! Radius!" and turns Hallemeier's "trefil byste na sto kroků škopek?" (could you hit a tub/noggin at a hundred paces?) into the neutral "Could you get him from here".

cut

Busman's elegy cut

Čapek, 1920
Vidíš, Busmane, žádný král neměl větší mohyly než ty. Půl miliardy na srdci--Ach, vždyť je to jako hrst suchého listí na zabité veverce, ubohý Busmane!

“You see, Busman, no king ever had a greater burial mound than you. Half a billion on your heart--Ah, it's like a handful of dry leaves on a killed squirrel, poor Busman!”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
(cut)

Gall's grotesque-tender epitaph, Domin's "byl to svým způsobem hrdina... Plač, Heleno!" (he was a hero in his way... Weep, Helena!) and Fabry's ironic "génius financí" all go. Only Hallemeier's "All honor to him" remains, so Busman's death becomes a plot event rather than a mourned one. "S půl miliardou na srdci" (on his heart) is also flattened to "by his side".

cut

Nana's Judgement Day and Helena's farewell cut

Čapek, 1920
(zleva se otevřou dveře, v nich stojí Nána) Nána: Na kolena! Přišla hodina soudu! ... Dělejte pokání, nevěřící! Je konec světa! Modlete se!

“(door opens on the left; Nana stands in it) Nana: On your knees! The hour of judgement has come! ... Repent, unbelievers! It is the end of the world! Pray!”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
(cut)

Nana's apocalyptic cameo, the only voice that frames the fall as divine judgement, is removed, and so are Helena's "Sbohem, vy všichni" and Fabry plucking a flower from the bouquet as he leaves. With the litany also gone, the act loses nearly all its religious register.

wordplay/names

Radius's coda trimmed; "robit" pun and "Je to Robot" lost

Čapek, 1920
Robot: Je to člověk. / Radius: Je to Robot. Pracuje rukama jako Roboti. ... Budeš robit. Budeš stavět.

“Robot: He is a human. / Radius: He is a Robot. He works with his hands like the Robots. ... You will drudge [robit]. You will build.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
ROBOT. He is a man! / RADIUS. He works with his hands like the Robots. ... RADIUS. You will work! You will build for us!

Radius's paradoxical reclassification "He is a Robot" is cut, and "robit" (archaic/dialect "to do forced labour", the root of "robot") becomes plain "work". Radius's will-to-power peroration ("Svět patří silnějším. Kdo chce žít, musí vládnout... Vláda nad hvězdami! ... Místo, místo, víc místa pro Roboty!") and Alquist's warning "Zahynete bez lidí!" (You will perish without humans!) are also gone.

stage direction

The Robots march on the audience

Čapek, 1920
Radius: Není lidí. Roboti, do práce! Marš! / OPONA

“Radius: There are no humans. Robots, to work! March! / CURTAIN”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
They form in two lines, turn to audience and march mechanically to the footlights. As they are about to step over the footlights, as if into the audience, all LIGHTS go out.

Čapek's "Marš!" sends the Robots to work. The acting edition turns it into a military "march", with arms "vibrating high in the air" and a blackout as they seem to invade the stalls. That is a spectacular ending of its own that the Czech does not have. Elsewhere it adds cues such as "WIND machine begin" and "WARN Curtain".

Section 09: Dějství třetí part 1/2 ↔ English Epilogue

Czech from the act's opening stage direction and Alquist's monologue ("Nenajdu? -Nepochopím?") to his hand-washing ("Jak se jmenuješ?"); English from the Epilogue's opening through Alquist falling asleep and Primus and Helena entering ("Oh, Lord, let not mankind perish from the earth").

The English keeps the argument (the Robots demand Rossum's formula, Alquist is pushed into vivisection) and cuts away almost everything else: Alquist's lyrical despair, the Robots' claim to have souls, and the whole offstage vivisection with its screams and blood. Damon is removed and Radius absorbs his role, the horror is turned into choreographed stage business, and a few lines are added that make the Robots' collapse statistical and Alquist's conscience explicit ("murder").

reassignment

Damon removed; Radius becomes spokesman and vivisection victim

Čapek, 1920
Alquist: Kdo je to? / 5. Robot: Já, Damon. ... Alquist: Cože, ty to tedy chceš? -- Do pitevny s tebou! ... Damon: Já--proč právě já?

“Alquist: Who is that? / 5th Robot: I, Damon. ... Alquist: So that is what you want? Into the dissecting room with you! ... Damon: I... why me in particular?”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
RADIUS. Through me, the governments of the Robots of the world commands you to deliver up Rossum's formula. ... RADIUS. I? Why should I be chosen?

Damon "from Le Havre", the new Robot government's envoy, is dropped (the servant's "přijel Damon z Havru" becomes "just arrived from abroad"), and all his lines, his "read history" speech included, go to Radius, who is then the one dissected. The English Radius, leader of the revolt, is the one who suffers for it; in the Czech the vivisected Robot is an anonymous functionary.

cut

The offstage vivisection and its aftermath cut

Čapek, 1920
Křik Damonův: Aááá! / Hlas Alquistův: Držte! držte! / Křik Damonův: Aááá! / Hlas Alquistův: Nemohu! / Křik Damonův: Řež! Řež rychle!

“Damon's scream: Aaah! / Alquist's voice: Hold him! hold him! / Damon's scream: Aaah! / Alquist's voice: I can't! / Damon's scream: Cut! Cut quickly!”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
ALQUIST. (_Off R._) God, give me strength. (_Comes on, horrified_) No, no. I will not. I cannot. (_Sits R.C._)

About two pages of Grand Guignol are gone: the screams, the victim begging to be cut, Alquist's bloody coat, Damon staggering out in a bloody sheet saying "je lépe žít" (it is better to live), and Alquist's speech to his "krvavé pařáty" (bloody claws). This also removes Damon's discovery that a Robot wants to live, which in the Czech is the first sign of what Primus and Helena will show.

stage direction

Primus and Helena's entrance moved, and Alquist falls asleep

Čapek, 1920
(Roboti Primus a Helena vběhnou středem) / Helena: Prime, Prime, co se děje? Kdo to křičí? ... Helena: Ne, ne, ne! (zakrývá si oči) Je to hrrozné!

“(The Robots Primus and Helena run in through the centre) / Helena: Primus, Primus, what's happening? Who is screaming? ... Helena: No, no, no! (covers her eyes) It's horrible!”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
(_Falls asleep, and after the count of ten, PRIMUS and HELENA, hand in hand, enter L. and go to R.C.; look at ALQUIST._) / HELENA. The man has fallen asleep, Primus.

In the Czech the couple burst in during the vivisection and Helena nearly faints at the screams, so her feelings show first as horror and nausea. The acting edition invents a sleep with a timed pause ("count of ten") and brings them in "hand in hand", so they are a romantic couple from their first moment. Alquist's hand-washing scene with Helena fetching water is cut too.

cut

Alquist's lyrical opening monologue reduced to a few lines

Čapek, 1920
Ochlaď, ach ochlaď mi čelo, stará noci! ... noci, co tu chceš? Není milenců, není snů; ... Není lásky. Heleno, Heleno, Heleno!

“Cool, oh cool my brow, old night! ... night, what do you want here? There are no lovers, no dreams; ... There is no love. Helena, Helena, Helena!”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
If I could only sleep. Dare I sleep before life has been renewed? Night again. Are the stars still there? Of what use are the stars?

The address to the night, the cry for the dead Helena Glory, and the long mirror scene ("Tváři, ubohá tváři! Podobo člověka posledního!" / Face, poor face! Image of the last man!) are all cut. What's left is plain prose; the added "Dare I sleep before life has been renewed?" makes Alquist driven rather than broken.

cut

The Robots' claim to souls and human parentage cut

Čapek, 1920
2. Robot: Stali jsme se dušemi. ... 3. Robot: Slyšte, ó, slyšte, lidé jsou naši otcové! ... to je jejich hlas! Jsme jejich synové!

“2nd Robot: We have become souls. ... 3rd Robot: Hear, oh hear, people are our fathers! ... that is their voice! We are their sons!”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
(cut)

The play's central metaphysical claim, that the Robots became souls "z hrůzy a bolesti" (out of horror and pain), is gone, along with Alquist's reply "Roboti nejsou život. Roboti jsou stroje." (Robots are not life. Robots are machines.) and his "nic není člověku cizejšího než jeho obraz" (nothing is more alien to man than his own image). The English Robots want only a production formula, and their move toward humanity is left entirely to the Primus–Helena love scene.

addition

Industrial statistics and a death toll added

Čapek, 1920
3. Robot: Znásobili jsme práci. Nemáme už kam dát, co jsme vyrobili.

“3rd Robot: We have multiplied our work. We no longer have anywhere to put what we have made.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
1ST ROBOT. ... We have obtained a billion tons of coal from the earth. Nine million spindles are running by day and by night. There is no longer room for all we have made.

The English adds figures (coal, spindles, "This we have accomplished in one year") and a countdown that isn't in this Czech text: "Eight million Robots have died this year. Within twenty years none will be left." These may come from another edition of the Czech than the Gutenberg one, but here they make the Robots' crisis a matter of numbers rather than of the "Padá na nás hrůza" (horror is falling on us) and "měj slitování" (have mercy) the Czech gives them.

meaning shift

Alquist recast as an unlearned builder

Čapek, 1920
Alquist: ... Neudržím skalpel. Vidíš, jak mi slzí oči? Neviděl bych na vlastní ruce. Ne, ne, já nemohu!

“Alquist: ... I can't hold the scalpel. Do you see how my eyes water? I couldn't see my own hands. No, no, I can't!”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
ALQUIST. I tell you I cannot. I am only a builder. I work with my hands. I have never been a learned man. I cannot create life.

The Czech Alquist refuses because he is too old and tearful, and because "ze zkumavek předpis života nevyjde" (the formula of life won't come out of test tubes). The English adds a statement of class and craft ("only a builder", "never been a learned man") that points ahead to the ending's humanism, and it drops his tears and blurred sight.

meaning shift

Conscience made explicit: "murder" added

Čapek, 1920
Alquist: Živá těla? Cože, já je mám zabíjet? Já, který jsem nikdy-- ... Bože, aby to nebylo nadarmo!

“Alquist: Living bodies? What, I am to kill them? I, who have never-- ... God, let it not be in vain!”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
ALQUIST. Am I to commit murder? ... If only this murder is not in vain.

The Czech uses "zabíjet" (to kill) and the half-sentence "I, who have never--"; the English twice says "murder", the word for killing a person, so the dissection becomes a moral crime against a person. An added closing prayer, "Oh, Lord, let not mankind perish from the earth", makes the scene more piously Christian, though the Czech's religious passage, about the dead reaching out "z onoho světa" (from the other world) with hands full of life, is cut.

tone/register

Threats and sexuality made blunter

Čapek, 1920
3. Robot: Nepovíš-li, zahyneš. Máme uloženo zabít tě. ... 3. Robot: Lidé nám nedali plemeniti se.

“3rd Robot: If you don't tell, you will perish. We have been instructed to kill you. ... 3rd Robot: People did not let us breed.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
RADIUS. Silence is punishable with death. ... 1ST ROBOT. You made us sterile. We cannot beget children.

The Czech threat is a plain order passed on; the English turns it into the formula of a state legal code. "Didn't let us breed" becomes the clinical and biblical "sterile ... beget children". Alquist's contemptuous "Jakže, věci, jakže, otroci" (What, things, what, slaves) is cut, so his "breed like animals" loses its scorn.

stage direction

Choreographed Robot movement in the stage directions

Čapek, 1920
(vejde výbor pěti Robotů)

“(a committee of five Robots enters)”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
(_Other ROBOTS remain two abreast at L.C., right foot forward._) ... (_Right hand up over ALQUIST. All ROBOTS' left arms still back._)

The Czech gives no movement. The Samuel French edition writes a mechanical choreography ("open hands, close when stopped", "ALL draw back one step in consternation"), which records how Playfair's production staged the Robots as machine-like, while the text that would have shown their inner life is cut.

10

Section 10: Dějství třetí part 2/2 ↔ English Epilogue

Czech from "Helena: Robotka Helena." (Alquist naming the servant Helena) to "OPONA"; English from "(Falls asleep, and after the count of ten, PRIMUS and HELENA...)" to "Adam--Eve. *Curtain*", with Alquist's dawn/sleep monologue partly folded into the Epilogue's opening speech.

The English keeps the skeleton of the Primus–Helena love scene and the dissection test but strips almost everything lyrical, religious and tender from it, ending the play on a two-word cue instead of Alquist's Genesis reading and Nunc dimittis. Helena's childlike strangeness (the spilled test tube, the birds, the mirror, her rolled "hrrozné") is flattened, and Alquist's motives are made explicit while his compassion is cut.

cut

The Genesis reading and Alquist's Nunc dimittis are cut; the play ends on "Adam--Eve."

Čapek, 1920
Jdi, Adame. Jdi, Evo; budeš mu ženou. Bud jí mužem, Prime. ... "A stvořil Bůh člověka k obrazu svému ..." ... Nyní propustíš, Pane, služebníka svého v pokoji

“Go, Adam. Go, Eve; you shall be his wife. Be her husband, Primus. ... "And God created man in his own image ..." ... Lord, now lettest thou thy servant depart in peace”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
ALQUIST. Go. (_Exit PRIMUS and HELENA L._) Adam--Eve. *Curtain*

The Czech ends with a marriage blessing, Alquist pouring out his test tubes, reading Genesis 1:27–31 ("the sixth day") and a long Simeon-style hymn that life "will not perish" through love. The acting edition drops all of it, including the religious frame and the explicit thesis of the play, leaving only the Adam/Eve allusion.

cut

Opening exchange and Alquist's dawn lament cut; sleep moved earlier

Čapek, 1920
Alquist: Helena? Proč Helena? Kdo ti tak dal říkat? / Helena: Paní Dominová. ... Proč ještě svítá! Ó-ó-ó, co chce nový den na hřbitově života?

“Helena? Why Helena? Who told you to call yourself that? / Mrs Domin. ... Why does day still dawn! Oh, what does a new day want in the graveyard of life?”

(cut)

The English never shows Alquist learning that the robot is named after Helena Domin, so the link between the two Helenas is lost; his despairing dawn speech is reduced to "If I could only sleep" in the Epilogue's first speech and he simply "Falls asleep" after the dissection of Radius (itself compressed from earlier Czech material).

cut

Helena's naive business with the test tube, the "secret", and the birds cut

Čapek, 1920
Helena: Nesahej na mne! (porazí zkumavku) Ach, teď jsem to vylila! ... Primus: Na to se nesmíš dívat, Heleno. To je tajemství. ... Ach, Prime, já bych chtěla být ptákem!

“Don't touch me! (knocks over a test tube) Oh, now I've spilled it! ... You mustn't look at that, Helena. It's a secret. ... Oh, Primus, I'd like to be a bird!”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
HELENA. I've seen him looking into this. ... PRIMUS. (_Examining the book_) That is the book the old man is always reading.

The Czech Helena is clumsy, flirtatious and impulsive (she spills Alquist's experiment, quarrels about whose fault it is, wants to be a bird). The English makes her a polite observer, and the "secret of life" becomes something Primus discovers on his own rather than a forbidden thing he warns her off.

cut

The mirror monologue cut; "hrrozné" lisp lost

Čapek, 1920
Ach, ty hrrozné vlasy ... (nakloní se k zrcadlu) Ty že jsi krásná? Proč krásná? Jsou krásné vlasy, které tě jenom tíží?

“Oh, this awful (rolled "hrr") hair ... (leans to the mirror) You, beautiful? Why beautiful? Is hair beautiful that only weighs you down?”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
HELENA. Am I beautiful? Of what _use_ is it to be beautiful?

Helena's speech to her own reflection, about hair, eyes, and lips "you only bite so it hurts", is condensed to one line, and the stage mirror vanishes. The doubled "rr" in "hrrozné / hrrozně", a mark of her girlish emphasis (and an echo of the human Helena's speech), has no equivalent.

meaning shift

"I'm good for nothing" turned into "What am I for?"

Čapek, 1920
Ne, jistě, já nejsem k ničemu; každý říká, že nejsem k žádné práci. Já nevím, jaká jsem.

“No, surely, I'm good for nothing; everyone says I'm no use for any work. I don't know what I am like.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
and when I am there in the garden I feel there may be something-- What am I for, Primus?

The Czech says Helena is a defective worker robot who doesn't know her own nature. The English turns this into an existential question about purpose and adds the half-line "I feel there may be something".

addition

Alquist's motive spelled out; his tenderness and bluff cut

Čapek, 1920
Alquist: Kdy byla udělána? / Primus: Přede dvěma roky. / Alquist: Od doktora Galla? ... Alquist: (zazvoní) Ach, Prime, jak je dávno, co jsem byl mladým člověkem! Neboj se, Heleně se nic nestane.

“When was she made? / Two years ago. / By Dr Gall? ... (rings) Ah, Primus, how long ago it is that I was a young man! Don't worry, nothing will happen to Helena.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
ALQUIST. What, you would protect her? Laughter--timidity--protection--I must test you further. Take the girl into the dissecting room.

Čapek leaves Alquist's purpose implicit (Gall's robots, "everything depends on it") and lets him reveal the bluff to the audience with a nostalgic aside. The English adds a line that tells the audience it is a test and cuts the aside, the stroking of Helena's hair, the memory of Mrs Domin's hair and "Jsi krásná, Heleno. Bylo by tě škoda" ("You are beautiful, Helena. It would be a pity").

stage direction

Primus's threats and offer toned down

Čapek, 1920
Primus: (uchopí těžkou třecí paličku) Hneš-li se, rozbiju ti hlavu! ... (rozhaluje kazajku) Řež tady, tady! ... řež do těchhle prsou, ani nevykřiknu

“(seizes a heavy pestle) If you move, I'll smash your head in! ... (opens his jacket) Cut here, here! ... cut into this chest, I won't cry out”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
PRIMUS. If you do, I will kill you. (_Steps toward ALQUIST._) ... PRIMUS. Sir, take me. I am made on the same day as she is. Take my life, sir.

The pestle, the bared chest and the physical offer to be cut are dropped, along with Alquist's "Pomalu, hochu. Ne tak marnotratně" ("Easy, boy. Not so extravagantly") and "Je těžko umírat" ("It is hard to die"). The violence becomes a generic line, and the acting edition adds blocking ("Step to ALQUIST") and a "WARN Curtain" cue in the middle of the scene.

tone/register

"Starý" becomes "Man"; robots' "ty" to Alquist lost

Čapek, 1920
Primus: (zadrží ji) Nepustím! (k Alquistovi) Nikoho, starý, nezabiješ!

“I won't let you! (to Alquist) You'll kill nobody, old man!”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
PRIMUS. (_To ALQUIST_) I will not let you. Man you shall kill neither of us.

Throughout, the robots say "pane" but use the informal "ty" with Alquist, and here Primus drops "sir" for the blunt "starý", a turn toward equality. The English "Man" (unpunctuated in the edition) reads as a formal, almost biblical address and inverts the point: the robot now calls the last human "Man".

meaning shift

Helena's suicide threat changed

Čapek, 1920
Helena: Já skočím z okna, Prime! Půjdeš-li tam, skočím z okna!

“I'll jump out of the window, Primus! If you go in there, I'll jump out of the window!”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
HELENA. If you go in there and I do not, I will kill myself.

The concrete, impulsive image (the window she watched the sunrise from) is abstracted to "kill myself", and the condition is lawyered ("and I do not"). The scene loses some of its rawness.

wordplay/names

"Robotka" → "Robotess"; "snoubenci" cut

Čapek, 1920
Alquist: Vy jste snoubenci? Lidé? Odkud se vracíte? ... Helena: Robotka Helena.

“Are you an engaged couple? People? Where are you returning from? ... Robot-girl (feminine "Robotka") Helena.”

Selver & Playfair, 1923
HELENA. The Robotess Helena. (_Shies away L._)

The ordinary Czech feminine suffix (-ka) becomes the coinage "Robotess", which sounds archaic or comic. Alquist's sleepy first guess that they are "snoubenci" (a betrothed couple), which looks ahead to the final marriage blessing, is cut along with that blessing.
